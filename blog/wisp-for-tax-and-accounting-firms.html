Does Your Tax or Accounting Firm Need a WISP? What the IRS and FTC Expect

If your firm prepares tax returns, a written information security plan is not optional. Smaller firms have limited exceptions, not a blanket release from security duties.

The short answer

Yes, if you prepare tax returns. The IRS says tax professionals “are legally required to have a written, accessible plan and should review, test, and update it regularly” (IRS IR-2026-92). The FTC Safeguards Rule names tax preparation firms as covered businesses (FTC).

What applies to a smaller firm

Institutions maintaining customer information concerning fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from the specific requirements in 16 CFR 314.4(b)(1), (d)(2), (h) and (i) (16 CFR 314.6). That is not an exemption from maintaining a security program, assessing risks, or regularly monitoring and testing safeguards. Confirm the consumer count and applicable provisions with your adviser. Smaller covered firms still need:

  • A named Qualified Individual to run the program (this can be an outside provider)
  • Two-step login (MFA)
  • Encryption of client data
  • Staff training
  • Oversight of your vendors
  • Secure disposal of old data

Bookkeeping or accounting without tax returns

Any permitted alternative to encryption or equivalent access control in place of MFA must meet the rule’s approval conditions: the Qualified Individual approves effective encryption alternatives when encryption is infeasible, and must approve an equivalent secure-access control in writing for the MFA exception.

The rule may apply. It depends on whether your services count as financial activities. Tax preparation does.

If something goes wrong

Covered financial institutions must notify the FTC as soon as possible, and no later than 30 days after discovering a qualifying notification event involving unauthorized acquisition of at least 500 consumers’ unencrypted customer information. Encrypted information counts when its key was accessed; unauthorized access is presumed acquisition unless reliable evidence shows otherwise. Have your Qualified Individual and legal adviser assess the trigger and discovery date promptly. Other state, contractual, IRS or client obligations may apply even below that threshold. Reports may be made public (FTC). The IRS asks tax professionals to contact their Stakeholder Liaison right away so it can block fraudulent returns (IRS).

Where to start

The Qualified Individual maintains a dated control/evidence register and escalation contacts; leadership verifies that the WISP describes real controls and records test results. Do not wait for a completed forensic investigation to start evaluating reporting deadlines.

The IRS publishes a free WISP template for smaller practices, Publication 5708. The template is the easy part. The value is making it match what you actually do: who has access, where client files live, and what you do on a breach day. Your PTIN renewal also asks you to acknowledge this duty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do tax preparers need a WISP?
Yes. The IRS says tax professionals are legally required to have a written information security plan, and the FTC Safeguards Rule covers tax preparation firms.
Is there a free WISP template?
Yes. IRS Publication 5708 is a free template aimed at smaller practices.
Are small firms exempt?
The fewer-than-5,000-consumer exception covers only 16 CFR 314.4(b)(1), (d)(2), (h) and (i), not the entire security program, risk assessment or regular monitoring/testing. Confirm applicability with your adviser.
Can an outside IT company be our Qualified Individual?
Yes. The FTC confirms the Qualified Individual can be a service provider, though the firm keeps responsibility for oversight.
