Friday’s Ground Stop, Saturday’s Delays: Plan for the Recovery Tail

The FAA ground stop began November 13, not Saturday. What the weekend recovery teaches owners about dependencies, fallback work, and reopening safely.

First, get the dates right

The nationwide FAA ground stop began Friday, November 13, 2026, not Saturday. The FAA’s November 15 account says a technical problem occurred around 3:30 p.m. Eastern and air traffic resumed at 8:15 p.m. that Friday. Saturday, November 14 belongs in the story because the operational recovery continued into the weekend.

Reporting from Fox News described residual Saturday delays as airlines repositioned aircraft and crews. It also reported FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford’s Saturday assessment that the worst was behind them, with normal schedules expected by the end of Sunday. That was a forecast made during recovery, not a guarantee for any particular traveler. The FAA’s own account described a slow, steady return to normal operations.

What is known, and what remains an investigation

The FAA said instability affected communications and radar feeds, prompting a precautionary ground stop. It also said some frequencies and radar feeds remained reliable, controllers and pilots followed safety procedures, and aircraft landed safely. Calling this a total disappearance of air traffic communications would misrepresent the agency’s account.

In its initial investigation, the FAA identified two potential locations associated with excessive network traffic: a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and a radio communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas. It said its network provider, L3Harris, had been working at El Dorado, including deploying a router, and that isolating the locations stabilized the network. The agency said the root-cause investigation continued.

The FAA reported no indication of a cyberattack or outside malicious actor based on available evidence. That statement should be attributed, not expanded into a claim that every unanswered technical question is settled. There is no established causal link in the sources reviewed between this event, Thursday’s Microsoft disruption, and Monday’s bank issues.

The business lesson is the recovery tail

A Charlotte firm does not run an air traffic network, but it understands the same operational problem: a system can be available while the work is still behind. People are in the wrong place, appointments need rebuilding, and time-sensitive items are waiting. Restoring a connection is not the same as restoring a schedule.

For a traveling employee, verify the actual flight with the airline before reorganizing client commitments. For an office, apply the same habit to critical work: check the specific appointment, shipment, approval, or service request rather than relying on a general status banner. One recovered dependency does not prove that the entire chain is ready.

Keep a short list of work that can continue without the disrupted dependency. That might include preparing documents offline under approved controls, switching a client meeting to a verified phone call, or assigning another qualified employee to a time-sensitive task. Decide in advance who can authorize substitutions and who must approve a changed commitment.

Give someone responsibility for reopening

During a disruption, assign one person to maintain an exception list. Record the affected task, owner, next action, and next update time. Avoid distributing several competing spreadsheets or asking customers to explain the same issue to multiple people. A modest, accurate list beats a sophisticated dashboard nobody trusts.

When service returns, restart work in a sensible order. Resolve deadlines and customer commitments first, then routine backlog. Check whether an earlier retry already succeeded before submitting it again. Preserve normal approvals; urgency does not make a duplicate transaction or conflicting instruction harmless.

Technology changes also deserve a recovery plan before they begin. Ask your IT provider what constitutes success, what signals trigger rollback, and who has authority to stop the change. Test fallback arrangements without weakening authentication or bypassing safeguards. These are general continuity practices, not a claim that a small-office checklist could diagnose the FAA’s incident.

A practical exercise for this week

For your own workflow, the business owner—not only the technician—signs off after a representative transaction succeeds and the exception list is assigned. Set a stabilization observation window appropriate to the workflow before bulk restart.

Pick one workflow that depends on an outside provider. Walk through a scenario where that provider is unavailable and later returns with a backlog. Can staff find contacts, communicate safely, prioritize work, and confirm completion? Include the return to normal in the exercise, not just the first emergency call.

Owner takeaway

The technical stop can end before the operational disruption does. Plan for the backlog, not only the outage.
